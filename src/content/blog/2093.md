I've always enjoyed 2093 (album by rapper Yeat) from the get-go in 2024, and from what I understand is now a hot take on the internet, I think it still holds up in 2026. It's somehow become one of the more divisive records in his discography, perhaps because of what this release in particular represented to Yeat; this is his first "sober" album, coinciding with one of the wildest sound directions he's played around with. This left his older fans that enjoyed his previous aesthetic in albums such as 2 Alive and Up 2 Me wanting something familiar. As this was my entry point into Yeat's music, I couldn't complain. In fact on first listen, a couple of these songs would grow to become some of my favourite songs of all time.

## The instrumentation paints the scenario

But I'm not here to exclusively talk about my opinions on each track, or how it's my favourite Yeat album. This record recently re-entered my rotation last April/May, at the height of my hard work towards finishing my dissertation and exam revision. I listened to the tracks on repeat like clockwork and as time's arrow marched on, I started seeing things (in the record hahaha). As the album title would suggest, 2093 provides a **futuristic** mix of industrial hip-hop, rage, distortion and synths, all of which contribute two key themes this album makes known out the gate with the first track "Psycho CEO": Order and Chaos. The track's vocal layers droning in the back as the synths tote a towering a effect over the listener establish a cold and sharp setting to the album. It's not just a future the music is transporting us to: the arrangement paints a dystopia (That's right, I'm talking themes here baby).

I actually began my re-listen a bit before most of my work on my diss paper had begun, as it this dystopian musical aesthetic had become a strong impetus for one of the most vivid artistic dreams I wish to put to canvas. It was at this point where I had also begun to establish the dissonance between what the instrumentals paint in my head, and the messaging in the lyrics, which are surprisingly focused for a rage-rap album. Again looking back on that older Yeat aesthetic, what longtime fans, alongside I, enjoyed about that era was the "give no damns" "pour up my cup and count my money" swagger he toted. Like you could tell he was on something while making those records. The beats were far more minimalistic, and Yeat's refrains withheld clarity to give way to a more rager vibe.

## The lyricism paints the character

2093 transcends this with its cinematic synths and far clearer vocals (for the most part). Money and braggadocio is still here, but far FAR less prominent, instead giving way to portray this power-hungry, all-seeing, all-knowing character Yeat plays for the majority of the album. Tracks like the afforementioned "Psycho CEO", "lyfestyle" and "Team ceo" enforce this character. The knowledge laid out about the character throughout the album and promotion seem limited and is admittedly subjective. From my perspective, The CEO is the leader of a police surveillance state set in the year 2093. His lyrical focus on severing ties and being motivated by capital are not representative of being particularly human or empathetic, yet we the listeners must cling to this in the darker, more fleeting moments of the record as it's established he's the only perspective of relevance in this dystopia he has dominated. **The instrumentals provide insight into the world, while the lyrics develop the subject of the world we are envisioning this world from.** His perspective is what makes the bread & butter of some of the less popular tracks on the record. With the exception of the final track on the album and perhaps a couple of other tracks, we are thought to believe it's a desireable outcome to be at the top of this downbeat, heavily-controlled society, from how Yeat's bragging and viewpoint become our spyglass for a lot of this album's depiction of the future.

We also see introspection to be a more common theme on this record than any Yeat record prior: "Power Trip" (my favourite song on the album), "Bought The Earth", "Tell Me" and "Mr. Inbetweenit" sees Yeat address feelings such as paranoia, guilt, addiction, difficulties in personal relationships, as well as the motivation to right his wrongs through compelling lyricism. Again, a first for Yeat from what I'm aware, which makes it hard for me buy why there are 2093 haters that argue this change in focus is a negative. "Nothing Change" is a particularly concerning lament into how through living up till this point in the future, people do not change, seeing a monotony that was not necessarily caused by him assuming power in the future.

"ILUV" examines how his position (not necessarily in this dystopia, but also his position in rap culture at the time) is grounds for consumers to "get mad" at him, meanwhile his lyrics serve to spin that feeling back at his fans in a sort of goading way, asking how "they even understand what he's saying to them" and that "they love", resulting in a song that feels like two thunderclouds raging over each other. This translates back into the dystopia narrative the album paints; the CEO insisting to his workers that they love to follow his orders, wondering how they could even understand anything different. The theme of power struggle is not just met through the again surprisingly focused angle Yeat takes lyrically, but through the beat also. Sampling "Fleece" by Crystal Castles, it's a dark, hypnotic banger with its synths crashing over each other like waves. My second favourite from this record.

## The 3-track run of chaos & confusion

The midpoint of the tracklist is where we hit 3 of the most polarising tracks in the album, but there is a distinguishing quality to each of them that makes me really like them each. **Riot & Set it Off** is the chaos theme in full effect; chaos throughout the album is exhibited with power struggle, and this song excels in this notion. The strings swelling into a breakbeat make me think of a tense chase scene, outrunning a swarm of mind controlled henchmen. The driving beat in conjunction with the irregularity of Yeat's refrains create a tunnel effect: no sign of the effects closing in on theirselves. The second half drops the breakbeat suddenly in favour of a slower pace, like an explosion has suddenly been set... off... huh... While not favoured by many for being a farcry from what Yeat typically does (again a driving factor in longtime fans not enjoying the record), I applaud the imagery the instrumental provokes. The music video is also insane.

Oddly enough, this chaos **fully** dies down in the immediately following "Team ceo". This track features the CEO character more prominently, but the emphasis on power dynamic through a cinematic beat is swapped in for a far more lowkey, somewhat danceable beat. The robotic rhythm, with its verse and chorus repeated twice each throughout the song each to me represents the heteronomy felt on all the rungs lower to our character. It's as if all the lyrics on this song are being played over a communication system to the henchmen. While, in my opinion, the song dances around the top 10 songs on the album, I think it's still a wicked listen, and to me it represents the most order on the record.

The album's progression only gets more insane with the title track, "2093", rounding out this 3-track museum of oddities. This is arguably the least liked track on the entire album to many. My hot take? It's very very cool. It's also handily the darkest song on the album too. Instead the music portraying a fight for control looming over you (psycho ceo, iluv), or the CEO character's control over his surroundings (breathe, lyfestyle, bought the earth), "2093"'s muddy, static-like synths portray a complete loss of control, even from the viewpoint of Yeat. It's as if you're in-between feeling human and being plugged into the future, seeing only static for now; 2093 is unplugged. The lack of control from the narrator/Yeat/CEO (they're all interchangeable for this song honestly) is exemplified through his repeated refrain "I live in 2093" over and over again, and lyrics that depict the sensation of spinning out. Sounds like pure mind control. It's the most futuristic on the record, almost to a fault, sounding more like a proxy setting somebody experiences after acting out of line in dystopian fiction. The brainwashed lyricism is juxtaposed by a single line of bright, humanity-leaning imagery: "20 friends by the beach", before snapping back to the heteronomy and madness of this dystopia Yeat is in. So yeah, maybe I have overanalysed it, but thats just the joy of working like a machine, while listening to music about feeling like a machine.

## The Last Leg

I will admit the album lulls towards the end. I see where people come from when they say the album is overloaded, but in my opinion having only 2 average songs out of 22 is not a bad track record. The record lulls in the sense the production winds down, the album's theme of control and the focus on the CEO character fall to the wayside. "Stand On It" unfortunately represents this instance, being in my opinion a regular trap cut with an okay Future verse. It acts as a factory reset after the wild 3-track run before it, carrying a steady momentum until the epic finisher.

## 1093

I mentioned earlier that all of the tracks besides the final track are from the perspective of this all-powerful man: the CEO. I also stated that a common takeaway from these tracks is how the lifestyle serves to only benefit this character, who's supposedly seen it all and thrives at the top. This notion is swept to the wayside completely for "1093", the final track and among the best. It differentiates itself from its millenium ahead counterpart, backed by soft and atmospheric 80s-style synths. It feels warm and based within reality, as opposed to the entire rest of the album and its cold, dystopian demeanour. Here, the voice rapping feels like a regained perspective, not from Yeat in the future, but from present day. With the lyrics representing a mournful rejection of his future "CEO" self, which he debases to being a paranoid caricature, 1093 is a poignant and surprisingly emotionally intuitive internal monologue. "I can take you to the end, but I can't be your friend. Tried to show you the other way, but that's the way of dead men". The closer of the album being a regain of control in the present serves to be the only bright light amidst the forboding dystopia portrayed otherwise.

## Final Musings

So yeah maybe my overstressed uni student brain over-analysed a futuristic rap album, but if anything I have learnt I enjoy the theme of dystopia as a springboard for making some of my own art. I love this album for its ability to give me a spyglass into what 60+ years into the future could look like, what it would feel like being at the top of a civilisation that is so tightly optimised and monitored. I'm excited to see what COCOON and Reborn look like, after finding ADL somewhat okay. Give 2093 a listen if I what I've yapped about has intrigued you!

## Rating: 8.8/10

**Favourite Tracks:** Power Trip, ILUV, Breathe, 1093, Tell Me, If We Being Real, Riot & Set It Off, Bought the Earth, As We Speak (if we're counting P2)

**Least Favourite Tracks:** Stand On It, Run They Mouth
